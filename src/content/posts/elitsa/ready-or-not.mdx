After looking at the concept from all these angles, we wanted to know what it actually relies on.

## What do we actually believe?

Each of us wrote down questions and assumptions about our visitors, the technology and the event. We did this individually first, because we didn't want to influence each other and we wanted a bigger variety of ideas. It was also interesting to see what each of us was concerned about.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="805-6539" title="Individual questions and assumptions" caption="Each of us first wrote down questions and assumptions on our own." align="wide" />

After writing them all down, we noticed that many assumptions were about the same topics, and some even said almost the same thing. We grouped them into six main themes:

- Discovering the interaction
- Group dynamics
- Visitor flow
- Engagement and interaction
- Meaning and theme
- Technology and space

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="805-6540" title="Assumptions grouped into six themes" caption="Our assumptions grouped into six themes." align="wide" />

## Mapping the assumptions

Next, we placed every assumption in a matrix based on two questions:

- **Certain or uncertain:** do we have proof?
- **High or low risk:** how bad is it if we're wrong?

A high-risk assumption is part of the core of our concept. If it turns out to be wrong, the whole experience might not work. A low-risk one is something we can work around. Our focus was the top right corner: assumptions that are high risk, but that we have no proof for yet.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="805-6541" title="Assumption matrix" caption="Our assumption matrix with all our beliefs mapped." align="wide" />

## Digging into the research

For the assumptions in the matrix, we looked for research that could support them or prove them wrong. For every assumption we wrote down what we found, the source, and a conclusion about what it means for our concept.

Some of our beliefs turned out to be right. People notice that something is interactive within about a second if it reacts right away. A physical object, like a torch, invites people to interact instead of being a hurdle, for adults too. And when people interact with an installation, others come to watch and then join in, which is known as the honeypot effect.

Other beliefs turned out to be wrong. We thought people who come alone would still interact, just for a shorter time, but research showed that people on their own rarely interact with public displays. We also expected parents to let their kids play while they watch, but parents often try an exhibit themselves first. We marked these as disproved and kept them in mind for our design: the wall should work for groups, and the interaction should be fun for parents as well as kids.

We also found a few things we didn't expect. For older visitors, their grandchildren are often the strongest invitation to try something new. And strangers connect more easily when the installation itself gives them a reason to, which supports our idea of roots connecting between different people's lights.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="805-6731" title="Research on high-risk, uncertain assumptions" caption="Research on our high-risk, uncertain assumptions: insights, sources and conclusions." align="wide" />

## Some things you can't google

Not all assumptions can be answered with research. Some are about our concept specifically: whether a webcam can find torch light on our projected wall, whether our corner gets dark enough, or whether people understand the breathing rhythm as a reference to the theme. For these we made a validation plan. For each assumption we wrote down how we will check it, either with a test or by asking the client, and when. The results will be added to the table when we do our primary research.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="806-6779" title="Validation plan" caption="Our validation plan: what we test ourselves, what we ask the client, and when." align="wide" />

## A place for curiosity

Next to the assumptions, we also made a space for topics we were simply curious about, or that weren't part of the assumptions. Anyone in the team can add something there, do some research and write down their findings. For example, we looked into how plant-like structures can be generated with code, which will help us later when we start building the roots.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="806-6862" title="Curiosity table" caption="Our table for topics we are curious about." align="wide" />

## What we learned

Validation showed us that the core of our concept works well, but also that there is still a lot we need to test. The top right corner of the assumption matrix was fuller than we expected. Finding research that contradicted some of our beliefs was just as useful as finding research that supported them, because it showed us where our own expectations could be wrong.

## Next steps

With the concept validated, our next step is activation: how visitors notice the installation, how they start interacting, and how we make sure they want to join in.
