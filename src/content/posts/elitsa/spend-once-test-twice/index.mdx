## Buy a feature

Before testing our prototype, we wanted to know which features people find most important. So we did a buy a feature test. Every tester got $60 in play money and 14 feature cards, from roots growing from your light to projection on curtains. They could spend the money however they wanted and tell us why.

Most money went to the core: roots growing, connecting and flowers blooming, the breathing wall, and a torch that is already pointing at the wall. Extras like music and decorations got less, and nobody really wanted the coloured torches or the projection on curtains. One tester put $20 on hidden surprises, but also said they would spend an hour collecting every flower. That's way too long for a walk where people stay a few minutes.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="521-6233" title="Buy a feature board" caption="Our buy a feature board, with the money each tester spent and their notes." align="wide" />

## Why we stopped at three

We stopped after three people. The test was nice, and it confirmed what we already thought was important, but it didn't tell us much new. People rated ideas on paper or on screen, not the real experience, and some even said they put $1 somewhere just to use up their money. We'd rather see how people actually react to the wall, so we moved on to testing the experience itself.

## Round 1: an A/B test

For round 1 we tested scene 2 of our prototype: roots grow from your light, connect with other people's roots, and flowers bloom and breathe. One torch was already pointing at the screen, and testers could also use their phone flashlight. We gave them six tasks and a few extra questions.

We split the testers into two groups:

- **Group A** (3 people) heard the context first: the festival and the theme of breath.
- **Group B** (2 people) got no context at all.

This way we could see if the meaning comes across on its own, like it would for visitors walking past in the hall.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="1798-11265" title="Round 1 observation tables" caption="Round 1: the observation tables of group A (P1-P3) and group B (P4-P5), with our conclusions below." align="wide" />

## What we found

Both groups knew what to do within seconds. Everyone picked up a light without help, and group B even connected with other people's roots without being asked. So the interaction works.

The meaning didn't come across yet. Nobody saw the breathing on their own, not even with the context. There was too much blur and movement, so closing flowers looked like they were disappearing, and flowers often appeared in places that felt random. Both groups also said they'd rather play first and read a short text about the meaning after.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="1939-12604" title="To-change list after round 1" caption="Our to-change list, grouped by visuals, interaction, sign & meaning, sound and testing. The names show which testers it came from." align="wide" />

## Why no interviews

We didn't do interviews this time. Our installation is something people walk past and play with for a few minutes on one evening, so what matters most is how they react in that first moment. Watching people use it tells us more than asking them what they would want. Our client also asked for an experience that fits the theme, not a solution to a problem people have, so there weren't really needs to dig into. For teams with a client who wants a product people use often, like an app, interviews would make a lot more sense.

## Next steps

Next, we will need to work through our to-change list:

- less blur and movement
- a breathing rhythm you can read
- flowers that appear where roots really connect
- a short sign

Then we test again in round 2, this time with scene 1 and the wave. But first we really have to figure out the scene to even be able to code it :)
