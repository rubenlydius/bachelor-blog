With a theme as open as "Breath", it was tempting to jump straight into ideas. Instead, we first tried to understand the problem, using the methods we learned in our Experience Design classes.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="304-3247" title="Issue tree with How Might We questions" align="wide" ratio="16 / 7" />

## Issues and more issues

We started with the assignment itself as the top question and wrote it as a How Might We:

<Breakout>How might we give families and mixed-age groups a shared moment of wonder around "breath" during a winter walk at Transfo Intiem?</Breakout>

From there, we broke it down into smaller How Might We questions. The lower we went, the more concrete they became. We added hearts to the HMWs we thought had the most potential and gave us opportunities to think about the problem more in depth. We chose to focus on the word "feel" and look at all the possible interpretations it has.

<ImageRow>
  <Note>How might we let visitors feel the rhythm of the sound?</Note>
  <Note>How might we show breath as a feeling (calm, slowing down, being alive)?</Note>
</ImageRow>

## Ishikawa diagram

Next, we looked for the causes of the problem: "Why do installations at an event like this often fail to give a group a shared moment?"

<Breakout variant="plain">Interactive installations at winter walking events rarely give mixed-age groups a shared moment of wonder</Breakout>

We grouped the causes into audience, time and flow, theme, environment, interaction, and site and event. For example, kids can't read instructions yet, older visitors hold back from touching, around 300 visitors an hour means short stops, and the Pompenzaal echoes. Most causes turned out to be about the situation, not about breath itself.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="304-3248" title="Ishikawa diagram with the causes grouped per category" align="wide" ratio="16 / 8" />

One of the most surprising ones for us to realise was that single-user interactions create queues and can split groups up. It made us realise that the whole group should be able to join at the same time, yet the interactions should not take long, to avoid bottlenecks.

## Research question

We listed what we already knew and what we still wanted to find out, like how long visitors stay at one installation, and what breath means to a child, a parent, and a grandparent. Those open questions became our research to-dos. Then we converged and refined the research question in a few rounds.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="304-3429" title="What we already know and what we want to know" align="wide" ratio="16 / 6" />

In the end we ended up with a question that combined all the issues that could occur. With it we could tackle the interpretation of the theme, shared experiences for the whole group, how visitors feel, and the differences in age and ability.

<Breakout>How can an interactive installation that interprets the theme "Breath" create a shared moment of wonder and calm for families and mixed-age groups (5-80) during a short stop on the winter evening walk of Transfo Intiem?</Breakout>

## From "visible" to "interpret"

Our first HMWs were all about making breath visible. Halfway through, we realised this already chose a concept for us, while the assignment asks us to interpret the theme. So we picked the strongest questions from the issue tree and combined them into our refined HMW:

<Breakout>How might we interpret the theme "Breath" to create a meaningful experience that a whole group, from a 5 year old to an 80 year old, can join at once, understand, and feel?</Breakout>

## What we learned

The problem space was smaller than in our earlier projects, because the client had already decided the event, the place and the theme. So it was less about finding "the" problem and more about the situation. Our refined HMW keeps the interpretation open for ideation, but is clear about who we design for.

## Next steps

Now we knew what the problem was, but who exactly are we designing for? Next up: our target audience.
