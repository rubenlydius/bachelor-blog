After defining the problem and our target audience, we still had a lot of open questions. What did the previous editions look like? What do we know about the Pompenzaal? And what is there to know about breathing, apart from the fact that we all do it? So we did some desk research, and it went a bit deeper than we planned.

## Getting to know Transfo

We started with the event and the site. We looked at the programmes of the two previous editions of Transfo Intiem, the history of the power station, the info about the Pompenzaal, and even the weather in December. For every source we wrote down notes and what they mean for our installation.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="367-3473" title="Desk research on the event and the site" caption="Desk research on the event and the site: sources, notes and what they mean for us." align="wide" />

A few things were worth mentioning, because they made us think. The first edition in 2024 had more than 3,000 visitors, twice the number in the brief. In 2025 there was already a light installation that moved like breathing, so a "breathing light" on its own would not be new. And the old power station used to breathe, in a way, with steam, pumps and cooling water. When the site got its new purpose, it was even described as giving the buildings "een tweede adem".

## More than just in and out

Next, we looked into the theme itself. One of us had read about "belly breathing" before, so we wanted to check that out. We read research specifically about it, but also about slow breathing, heartbeats, breathing together and how children breathe. Some of it surprised us. Breathing slowly, around six breaths a minute, really calms the body down, and a long exhale works even better. In one study, a single minute of slow breathing was already enough to calm children down, at camps, in a museum and on a playground. And when people breathe or move in sync, they feel more connected as a group.

<FigmaEmbed src={board} node="367-3474" title="Secondary research table" caption="Our secondary research table. Green conclusions support a direction, pink ones are a warning." align="wide" />

One of our initial questions was whether we could see people's breath in the cold, and track it. We looked into why you can see your breath in the cold, and found out it usually only happens below about 7°C. For option 1, the inside installation, the Pompenzaal is heated, so that idea was quickly shut down.

## What others already made

We also looked at existing installations about breath and heartbeat. In Pulse Room by Rafael Lozano-Hemmer, light bulbs flash with the heartbeat of visitors. In the Breathing Pavilion by Ekene Ijeoma, glowing pillars slowly pulse, and people breathe along. We noticed two kinds of installations: ones where the visitor's own breath or pulse drives it, which often means one person at a time and a queue, and ones that set a rhythm for everyone to follow. With possibly around 300 visitors an hour, that difference matters a lot for us: more people need to be able to interact at the same time.

## From notes to insights

All this research was a lot to keep in our heads, so we summarised it into eight insight cards. Each card has what we observed, what it means for us, and where it came from. For example, "a short stop" means the installation has to make sense within seconds, and "togetherness is the point" means more people should make it better, not slower. These cards became the base for our design principles.

## What we learned

We went into the research to answer some questions, and came out with a few we didn't expect. Some findings closed doors, like tracking visible breath inside. Others opened new ones, like the idea of a site that used to breathe. It also showed us that breath is not only about air, but also about rhythm, calm and being together.

## Next steps

With our insights ready, we turned them into design principles and started brainstorming. Next up: ideation.
